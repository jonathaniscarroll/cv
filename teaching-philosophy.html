Statement of Teaching Philosophy

Our gatherings in class primarily serve the purpose of creation and learning. They exist as a platform for us to explore territory that is often emerging or underexplored, whether through augmented reality, video game design, mixed reality performance, or other technological mediums. Our exploration doesn’t stop at just learning the tools themselves; it extends to figuring out what distinctive opportunities and possibilities these media offer as artists.

However, we gather not only for the technological aspect, but also to reinforce our purpose and power as artists. We apply these technologies to our art in distinctive ways, leveraging the unique advantages they offer, leading to the creation of artworks that are strikingly different from others. The primary question at the heart of our interactions is: “How can we as artists intervene with technology to produce what otherwise wouldn’t exist?” Often, the answer includes producing work that presents technology as a means of critique, offering a different perspective on how it influences our society and us as individuals. It can also be considered a lens through which we express ideas or thoughts that cannot be conveyed without the unique capabilities of the technology in question.

These are the two main ideals that I aim to instill in groups of artists engaging with technology. I view this as a way to empower them in their craft. In addition to the emphasis on technology and creativity, I also consider the labor of working with technology as a relevant topic in our class. We recognize the importance of dedicated effort and time spent in sustained creative practice, including the problem-solving, failure, and experimentation that are often invisible parts of artistic processes. It is these embedded values, effort, and exploration that contribute to the full experience of our gatherings.

Pedagogical Approach

My teaching is fundamentally grounded in horizontal relationships and collaborative learning. Rather than positioning myself as an authority dispensing knowledge, I create classroom structures where students experience themselves as powerful agents in their own learning. This is particularly important when teaching technical subjects that can feel intimidating. I frame these materials in ways that excite people, presenting contexts for work where students can visualize the end result, eliminating the “fog of war” and clarifying the path from concept to completion.

I believe in starting small with achievable first projects that students can develop as deeply as they wish. The trick is providing guideposts for those who want to go further without making those who don’t feel like failures. By providing tools and frameworks, students learn to take projects in their own direction, drawing on their own experience and interests. This approach proves particularly powerful in virtual learning contexts, where traditional studio sociality can be hard to achieve. In my experience co-teaching mixed reality and game art courses during the pandemic, I found that smaller exercises with clear frameworks actually enhanced rather than diminished student engagement and creative ambition.

Integration of Theory and Practice

My teaching weaves together theoretical frameworks with embodied, hands-on learning. In my courses at the University of Regina, Mixed Reality Performance Art (ART 290AL) and Video Game Essays (MAP 200AM), students simultaneously develop technical skills (building AR experiences, creating games) while engaging with critical theory about new media, digital performance, labor, and representation. For instance, in Video Game Essays, students study McKenzie Wark’s Gamer Theory and Ian Bogost’s How to Do Things with Videogames while creating their own experimental games using GB Studio. In Mixed Reality Performance Art, students engage with Rosalind Krauss, Safiya Umoja Noble, and performance theorists while creating live-streamed performances with custom augmented reality filters. This integration ensures that technology is never presented as neutral; rather, students learn to ask critical questions about what technologies do, what they enable, and what they obscure.

Equity, Diversity, Inclusion, Accessibility & Decolonialization

The approach I take to EDI&A in my teaching begins with a fundamental philosophical shift: rather than starting from a place of “how do I meet EDI&A criteria,” I start with students asking interesting questions they want to explore through their artwork. That creates organic space to think about how these topics relate to their work. If you’re asking the right kinds of questions, aspects of these themes will inevitably come into the work.

Creating Brave Space Through Horizontality

A key part of my pedagogy is cultivating vulnerability in the classroom, which relates directly to equity and inclusion. This means creating a horizontal dynamic where students feel on the same level as the instructor: a brave space. The question becomes: how do you raise somebody up in a classroom so they feel as powerful as you are, the teacher? This is especially important when teaching technical subjects that can be intimidating.

I address this through several concrete strategies: framing material in ways that excite rather than overwhelm people; presenting contexts where students can visualize end results; providing clear pathways from concept to completion; and offering choice in how deeply to engage with different aspects of a project. The structure itself becomes a form of accessibility: when students understand the architecture of their learning, they feel more agency and less alienation.

In virtual teaching contexts, I’ve found that this horizontal approach is strengthened by building in multiple ways of participating and contributing. In my co-taught courses, I emphasize that not all creative labor looks the same. A student might contribute through code, documentation, conceptualization, writing, or curation. This multiplicity of roles acknowledges different strengths and makes space for different modes of engagement.

Collaborative Design and Community

My teaching practices extend collaborative design beyond individual courses. My co-curation of the Ender Gallery (a Minecraft artist residency in partnership with the MacKenzie Art Gallery) emerged directly from conversations about accessibility and community in digital spaces. By working with co-curators Sarah Friend and Cat Bluemke, we deliberately chose Minecraft as a platform that has garnered mass appeal and remains relatively untouched by the art world’s gatekeeping impulses. The residency invited artists from diverse backgrounds to propose projects; the resulting exhibitions, ranging from queer and trans intimacies to Indigenous land-based practices to labor and creativity, demonstrated what becomes possible when technology is positioned as a tool for community expression rather than commodity.

This experience informs my classroom practice. When I co-teach, I consciously model collaborative decision-making. In both Mixed Reality Performance Art and Video Game Essays, students see us (myself and my co-instructor) making choices together about curriculum, responding to student needs, and troubleshooting technical problems collectively. This models a form of horizontal pedagogy that extends beyond the student–instructor dynamic.

Decolonial Perspectives in Digital Art

I’m committed to centering Indigenous voices and land-based knowledge in digital art contexts. One example is Simon M. Benedict’s contribution to Ender Gallery, Odanak: At the Village, which extended the default Minecraft environment to incorporate medicinal plants from the Odanak Abenaki community, alongside recreated buildings that tell stories of Indigenous educational and cultural institutions. By positioning Minecraft as a tool for Indigenous knowledge-sharing and land acknowledgment (rather than uncritical world building), the project challenged the default narrative of digital spaces as placeless or culturally neutral.

In my teaching, this commitment appears in my selection of readings, case studies, and artists. I prioritize works by Indigenous artists, queer and trans artists, and artists from communities historically excluded from art world institutions. When teaching about video games and virtual worlds, I engage with how these technologies are implicated in settler colonial worldbuilding (as McKenzie Wark and others have theorized). The goal is to help students develop critical digital practices that are in dialogue with, rather than extractive from, the communities and lands they inhabit.

Research Statement

My art practice privileges research as both a primary source of inspiration and a motivating impetus, appearing in forms both theoretical and practical. In practice, my research is an embodied aspect of my creative process, integral to the iterative development of my artworks. In theory, it manifests as an aleatoric engagement with texts found in libraries, archives, and thrift stores, often articulating an engagement with the specific place I’m working in.

Place and Land as Research Trajectories

Place and land are key trajectories of my research, informing the direction of my projects and often causing me to work in response to the history of where I’m living. My current major research-creation project, The Land Speaks: An Augmented Reality Narrative for Health and History in Industrial Cape Breton, exemplifies this approach. The project addresses a critical gap in public health communication: the failure to make legible the connections between historic industrial pollution and present-day autoimmune disease clusters. In Cape Breton, sanitized narratives of the steel industry obscure what Rob Nixon calls “slow violence”: the gradual, often invisible toxic exposure that produces health inequities in communities that lack the stories needed to understand their own suffering.

This work draws on my family’s own cluster of autoimmune illnesses as a case study. Through guided story sessions with the women in my family (who serve as co-creators and primary knowledge users) and archival research from the Beaton Institute, I’m developing a geolocated browser-based Augmented Reality experience in Sydney, Nova Scotia. The artwork uses narrative agents, such as a toxic dust particle or a household curtain, to translate complex scientific and historical relationships into accessible public dialogue. By integrating community narrative, environmental health research, and digital art, the project offers a novel knowledge translation mechanism that makes invisible ties between industrial history and embodied illness newly perceptible.

Collaboration and Co-Creation as Research Practice

Another significant form that my research takes is collaboration. Through co-creation, I can practice theory acquired in my readings regarding labour and social practices, as well as develop technical skills in collaboration with others as a mode of education. A productive mode of collaboration I’ve adopted from the tech world is the “game jam,” wherein a video game or digital art project is created collaboratively within a compressed timeframe, requiring rapid iteration, flexible roles, and shared problem-solving.

This collaborative approach extends to my curatorial work and teaching. The Ender Gallery residency exemplified research-through-collaboration, where the act of selecting four artists and creating exhibition conditions became a form of critical research into how proprietary platforms shape artistic practice. As I wrote with co-curators Sarah Friend and Cat Bluemke, we were interested in making “an epistemological land grab” around the term “metaverse,” using curation as a form of research into what artistic practices become possible when you take a platform like Minecraft seriously as a site for creative intervention. This experience has directly shaped my teaching, where I now consciously frame course design itself as collaborative research.

Integration of Spiritual and Technical Research

An emerging dimension of my research practice involves integrating spiritual and esoteric frameworks with technological development. Through engagement with practices like sigil-making and animistic approaches to land-based art, I’m exploring how belief systems can structure creative practice in generative ways. This integration appears in my teaching through readings and discussions about spiritualism, mediumship, and how contemporary technology both shapes and is shaped by contemporary spirituality. The goal is to move beyond viewing technology as purely material or rational, instead recognizing the ways it participates in world-making that includes the immaterial, the numinous, and the speculative.

Dissemination and Public Scholarship

The goal of my research is always some form of dissemination of the knowledge gained through the resulting artwork, at minimum to create conversation about the topic covered. Rather than remaining within academic or art-world silos, I’m committed to making my research accessible and actionable for the communities it concerns. In my work on industrial health impacts, this means deploying the AR artwork in Sydney itself, gathering public feedback, and iteratively refining it based on how people interact with it. In my teaching, it means designing courses that model public scholarship and community engagement as core to artistic practice.

Courses Developed and/or Modified

Mixed Reality Performance Art (ART 290AL)

University of Regina, Faculty of Media, Art, and Performance · Summer 2021 (with co-instructor Cat Bluemke) · Remote, synchronous via Zoom · 7.5 hours/week (2.67 credit hours equivalent)

Course Description

In this course students learn methods of augmented and virtual reality in creating embodied performance art works streamed to audiences on the Internet. The course explores the critical and historical contexts of both new media art and performance art while developing hands-on skills in Open Broadcasting Software, Snap Camera, Lens Studio, and related AR/VR tools.

Course Outcomes

  1. Have a basic understanding of the critical and historical contexts of both new media art and performance art.
  2. Demonstrate working knowledge of technologies applicable to mixed reality performance (OBS, Snap Camera, Lens Studio, VRChat, etc.).
  3. Create embodied performance works that engage critically with technology, the body, duration, and audience participation.
  4. Develop skills essential to artistic practice: creative exploration, critical thinking, self-directed learning, time management, project development, group critique participation, feedback exchange, and artist statement writing.

Key Pedagogical Features

  • Virtual studio practice: despite remote delivery, the course maintains intensive studio engagement through weekly technical demonstrations, in-class experimentation, and live performance elements.
  • Theory-practice integration: students engage with readings in digital performance history, media theory, and performance studies (Krauss, Goffman, Dixon, Noble) while creating live-streamed performance work.
  • Progressive complexity: three exercises build toward a final project, allowing students to experiment with discrete technical skills before integrating them.
  • Multiple performance contexts: students work with recorded performance, live streaming, and audience interaction, learning how technology shapes performative possibilities.

Course Materials and Readings

  • Dean, Aria. “Closing the Loop.” The New Inquiry, 2016.
  • Dixon, Steve. Digital Performance: A History of New Media in Theater, Dance, Performance Art, and Installation. MIT Press, 2015. (excerpt)
  • Goffman, Erving. The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life. Harmondsworth, 1978. (excerpt)

Software (all free): Open Broadcaster Software (OBS), Snap Camera, Lens Studio, Restream (browser-based), VRChat, Unity (for advanced technical work).

Video Game Essays (MAP 200AM)

University of Regina, Faculty of Media, Art, and Performance · Spring/Summer 2022 (with co-instructor Cat Bluemke) · Remote, synchronous via Zoom · 9 hours/week (3 credit hours)

Course Description

Mix philosophy, theory, and critique with interactive art production while learning to envision, design, and create a video game. Students explore artistic and critical contexts of art games while developing skills in game design and development using free, accessible tools.

Course Outcomes

  1. Have a basic understanding of the critical and historical contexts of art games.
  2. Demonstrate working knowledge of game development technologies applicable to art game creation (GB Studio, Aseprite, Piskel, Photoshop).
  3. Show progress in skills necessary for artistic practice: creative exploration, critical thinking, self-directed learning, time management, project development, critique participation, feedback exchange, and artist statement writing.
  4. Create original game projects that engage critically with representation, narration, perception, and the affordances of game engines.

Key Pedagogical Features

  • Accessible game development tools: GB Studio (no-code) removes barriers to entry while remaining creatively powerful.
  • Theory-practice integration: students engage with game theory (Bogost, Wark, Custodio) while creating experimental games.
  • Progressive exercise design: three exercises introduce discrete concepts (perception/remix, narration/text, representation/gallery space) before students propose final projects.
  • Critique as pedagogy: group critiques give feedback on both technical execution and conceptual ambition, modeling peer learning.

Course Materials and Readings

  • Bogost, Ian. How to Do Things with Videogames. University of Minnesota Press, 2011.
  • Custodio, Alex. Who Are You?: Nintendo’s Game Boy Advance Platform. MIT Press, 2020.
  • Wark, McKenzie. Gamer Theory. Harvard University Press, 2009.

Software (all free): GB Studio (no-code game builder), Piskel (browser-based pixel art), Aseprite (optional advanced pixel art tool).

Assessment Structure

AssessmentWeight

Assignments

  • Exercise 1: Perception. Students remix the GB Studio tutorial scene to explore perception, considering the roles of the obvious, the implied, and the unseen. This introductory exercise teaches the tool while engaging creatively with a structured framework.
  • Exercise 2: Narration. Students create an original project that explores narration using only text, with explicit attention to the unreliable narrator, and to how games convey meaning through language.
  • Exercise 3: Representation. Students create an original project that explores representation and/or its memory through the creation of an art gallery, considering tensions between high and popular art, the role of identity and outsiders, and whose art is displayed, how it’s framed, and who gets to curate.
  • Final Project. Students propose and develop an original project using at least five techniques covered in class (remixing assets, collage, pixel art, hyper-stylization, RPG mechanics, text-based gameplay, found assets, game music). Proposals are approved by instructors two weeks before the deadline.

Revisions Since Initial Delivery

  • Refined exercise prompts with more specific theoretical reference points (unreliable narrator, representation/memory, high/popular art tensions).
  • Clarified submission protocols: executables submitted as URLs before class, with a 24-hour advance upload window to prevent connectivity issues during critiques.
  • Expanded plagiarism policy language, including self-plagiarism and student responsibility for academic integrity.
  • Enhanced grading transparency with clear expectations for each assessment category.
  • Added a resource section for independent game developers (engine tutorials, online communities, continued learning).
  • Structured project approval through a two-week pre-deadline proposal review, improving feasibility and reducing student stress.

VRIM (Virtual Reality Interactive Music)

A multi-user software for creating spatialized sound collages for VR, mobile, and desktop platforms. Initially designed for the Meta Quest, VRIM uses the headset’s microphone to sample ambient sounds (singing, natural noises, audience input) which are spatially anchored and algorithmically composed into evolving soundscapes.

  • VRIM Island (residency at Gibraltar Point Art Centre, Toronto Island): documentation of generative compositions from ambient environmental samples.
  • VRIM Tar Ponds (early development): uses oral testimony of family members as source for generative compositions overlaid on the landscape of Sydney’s former tar ponds site.

Writing Sample

“Minecraft to Metaverse: A Year of Experimental Art and Curation Inside Proprietary Platforms”

Co-authored with Sarah Friend and Cat Bluemke · BlackFlash Magazine, October 2022

This publication documents our year curating Ender Gallery, an artist residency program hosted within the game Minecraft. The piece reflects on questions central to my teaching and research: how do proprietary platforms shape artistic practice? What does it mean to work critically within game engines and metaverses? How can curation function as research?

The essay traces four artist projects: Cat Haines’ (g)Ender Gallery (queer and trans intimacies through monumental VR-like builds), Simon M. Benedict’s Odanak: At the Village (Indigenous medicinal plant knowledge and community institutions), Huidi Xiang’s How to Be an Artist in Minecraft (labor, creativity, and artistic practice within game constraints), and Travess Smalley’s Chance Language Resource Pack (procedural generation and concrete poetry practices).

  • Platform literacy as artistic research: artists learned to “hack” Minecraft by remixing assets, creating custom resource packs, and manipulating in-game affordances, demonstrating creative agency within proprietary platforms.
  • Community as exhibition context: artists used Minecraft to create spaces for queer community expression, Indigenous knowledge-sharing, and public artistic dialogue rather than producing work for art-world gatekeepers.
  • Documentation practices: Minecraft-literate forms of documentation (the Ender Gallery Resource Pack Pack), since traditional photography and video don’t capture what’s at stake in these works.
  • Accessibility through platform choice: a game with mass appeal that remains relatively untouched by art world gatekeeping reaches audiences far beyond typical art world contexts.

This project directly shaped my teaching by demonstrating that learning platforms (game engines, AR tools, streaming software) can simultaneously be sites of creative resistance and modes of accessibility. Students in my courses encounter similar questions: how do we work critically within technological constraints? How do we build community? What forms of documentation honor different forms of creativity?

Teaching Philosophy in Practice

My teaching philosophy emerges most clearly in how I structure the experience of learning technical tools. I recognize that technology can feel alienating, especially to students without prior technical experience. Rather than presenting tools as neutral or inevitable, I position them as sites of critical inquiry. In Mixed Reality Performance Art, students don’t just learn how to use OBS and Lens Studio; they learn why these tools exist, whose interests they serve, and what alternatives might be imagined. Similarly, in Video Game Essays, students engage with how game engines encode particular ideologies about representation, narration, and agency.

This approach requires vulnerability from me as an instructor. I don’t present myself as possessing complete mastery; instead, I model the problem-solving and experimentation I’m asking students to do. When technical challenges arise in class, I treat them as teachable moments rather than failures. This helps students understand that debugging, iteration, and creative problem-solving are core to artistic practice with technology, not exceptional circumstances.

Notes and Reflections on Teaching Philosophy

This dossier represents my evolving understanding of pedagogy as inseparable from my artistic practice. Several key commitments run through both my teaching and my creative work:

  1. Horizontality as ethics. Whether in the classroom or in collaborative artistic projects, I work to dissolve hierarchies of knowledge and authority. This doesn’t mean abandoning expertise; it means sharing that expertise in ways that allow others to feel powerful and capable, modeling vulnerability, inviting questions, and treating technical problems as collaborative puzzles rather than individual failures.
  2. Technology as a tool for world-making, not world-replacing. I teach students to ask critical questions about what technologies enable and what they obscure. In AR, VR, and game engines, the “wow factor” of immersive media can obscure the political economies, environmental costs, labor conditions, and spiritual implications of these tools. Co-curating Ender Gallery reinforced this commitment: by engaging critically with Minecraft as a proprietary platform while still finding space for artistic agency within it, we modeled a form of critical technological engagement that I now bring to my teaching.
  3. Research as embodied, relational, and placed. Knowledge comes from specific bodies, specific relationships, and specific places. This shapes how I design assignments (asking students to think about their own lives, communities, and contexts), how I engage with collaboration, and how I think about what counts as valid research and artistic practice.
  4. Integration across apparent binaries. I work to integrate technical and conceptual learning, theory and practice, the spiritual and the technological, the personal and the political, in course design (pairing technical projects with theoretical readings), in assignment prompts (asking students to think about labor, representation, or community alongside code), and in collaborative structures that value diverse contributions.

Teaching, for me, is always an extension of artistic practice. Both are forms of world making, and both require vulnerability, careful listening, and the courage to ask interesting questions together.
